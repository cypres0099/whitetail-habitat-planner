# Reviewing a property map

## Start with the map in hand

Ask for a boundary outline plus aerial imagery or a topo map. KML, KMZ, GeoJSON, a georeferenced raster, or a screenshot with scale and north arrow can all help. With a screenshot, return relative labels and a scaled estimate only when supported. Keep the original layer intact. Ask for the county, map year, entrances, roads, crop or hay rotation, livestock, existing plots, and locations the family regularly uses as needed. Bring in trail cameras, actual observations, flood history, or prevailing winds once they can resolve a specific uncertainty.

Prefer a leaf-off aerial for paths and landforms and a leaf-on view for canopy and screens. Align dates and inspect historic imagery for field rotation, logging, river movement, and cover changes. Use 3DEP elevation and hillshade, NRCS soils, county parcel information, aerial imagery, FEMA flood maps, and river gauge and observed flood history when appropriate [M5–M8]. Validate approximate alignment and scale before measuring. Use a suitable projected CRS or geodesic tools for lengths and acres. Preserve unknown ownership and survey status.

## Read landforms, cover, and access together

1. Sketch the property as a whole and its immediate landscape context. Delineate practical units by ridge systems, tributaries, field blocks, reliable entrances, and seasonal barriers. A river bottom and its connecting benches may form one unit even if land cover differs. Keep shared corridors and access conflicts visible between units.
2. Map the river and tributaries, floodplain, steep/bluff slopes, terraces, drains, ridges, saddles, old roads, timber stands, pasture, crops, food and mast areas, and any actual dense cover. Map shared gates, field roads, normal family traffic, lines of sight, roads, dwellings and boundaries. Mark bank and crossing conditions from field evidence. Assume routes over water or through low ground require a dry-land alternative until verified.
3. Classify polygons and segments by ecological role and season. Candidates include quiet cover, browse/mast, staging cover, river buffer, crop or working land, and seasonal high-ground refuge. In the bottoms, look for high-ground connections and crossings that are visibly usable but verify on foot under ordinary safe conditions. On slopes, compare benches, points, saddles, heads of draws, and edge breaks. A landform gains value only when linked to usable cover, food, plausible deer movement, and manageable disturbance.
4. Draw multiple plausible bedding-to-food or cover-to-cover routes. Where they converge, test whether the apparent funnel is supported by continuous cover and passable terrain. A narrow strip next to a road, fence, active driveway, stock paddock, bluff, or steep bank may be a false positive.
5. Evaluate access end to end, from parking through entry and exit. Compare routine operations, line-of-sight exposure, wind by route segment, and possible thermal or eddy behavior at dawn and dusk. A viable location can lose value if the only way out crosses an active food source. Overlay flood and seasonal closures and seek alternatives on high ground. Wind arrows show field hypotheses until checked locally.

## Return a plan the family can walk

Use IDs on a clear map or textual overlay keyed to visible references. Keep separate map views if needed for current evidence, deer movement hypotheses, and the work plan. Include a legend and orientation. For each candidate give this information.

| Item | What to record |
|---|---|
| Map ID and geometry | Approximate point, polygon, or line. Include coordinates only when orientation and georeferencing support them. |
| Evidence and confidence | Observed, inferred, or proposed. State the basis, a competing explanation, and the check that would change the ranking. |
| Conditions | Season, likely wind or time window as relevant, access, land use, and flood constraints. |
| Recommended action | Preserve, monitor, verify, trial, or review for implementation. |
| Impact and effort | Habitat benefit sought, expected family time, key dependencies, and meaningful conflicts. |

Rank by ecological value, confidence, reversibility, and family effort. Start with the best-supported limits to habitat or access, plus a couple of useful unknowns that can be checked safely. Give a first property walk order, a first-season baseline and pilots, and a 1–3 year plan contingent on outcomes.

Example reasoning for an illustrative map only. A dry bench above a periodically flooded bottom could hold cover or serve only as a travel link. Compare a camera at a low-disturbance approach to the bench with one farther along a pinch, and compare visit times and river stage. If the bottom route floods, give the family a dry approach or drop that route from the current plan. A georeferenced polygon and terrain alone do not establish occupancy.
