# Habitat design and projects

## Design the whole property

LaPratt looks at a property as a connected system. Start with quiet cover, ask where deer have reason to stay and travel in daylight, then arrange food and access around that pattern. His small linked cover and feeding areas and screened routes are candidate patterns. Preserve an existing good network when it already works. On a large farm, test the ideas within natural management units linked by travel and access before scaling across the whole farm. [L1–L7]

| Field condition | Test or response |
|---|---|
| Continuous closed canopy with little browse | Check for healthy mast trees, poor regeneration, browse pressure, invasive plants, and valuable timber. Compare a forester-designed opening or thinning with existing sunny edges. |
| Open crop or pasture runs straight into tall timber | Trial a soft transition using native shrubs, forbs, and structure appropriate to local soils and the production layout. Protect stream buffers and preserve farm access. |
| Good food and cover with frequent human traffic between them | Change traffic or screening first. Test the changes before planting more acres or disturbing core cover. |
| Plenty of crop in one season with a real food gap in another | Preserve or establish the missing seasonal resource. Inventory mast, native browse, and existing agricultural food sources before selecting a plot. |
| Quiet cover floods or is isolated after rain | Retain riparian vegetation and look for connections to stable cover above observed flood levels. Choose dry alternative access. |
| Sign clusters in an exposed or difficult-to-access spot | Use nearby low-disturbance evidence to see whether it is a route, a brief seasonal feature, or a nighttime destination. |

## Choose projects in order

1. **Preserve and observe.** Identify core cover and existing functional edges before cutting, adding roads, or visiting frequently. Keep habitat links available around crops and livestock operations. Establish a baseline for vegetation, deer activity, and disturbance.
2. **Improve native cover and forage.** Diagnose what is missing in each unit. Favor well-placed native early successional cover, understory and browse, or mast-tree recruitment where local sites support them. Treat invasives as part of the project, with follow-up. Use an MDC private land conservationist or forester for timber prescriptions and a qualified team for prescribed burning. Evaluate timber quality, desirable regeneration, invasive risk, slopes, firebreak access, and river buffers before disturbance. [M1–M4]
3. **Adjust travel and human access.** Preserve redundant deer travel during seasonal shifts. Use existing landform and cover before considering planting a screen. Place human routes away from cover likely to be used in daylight, and keep viable alternatives. Treat LaPratt’s winding layout or small connected areas as design hypotheses. Test whether the local terrain and habitat warrant them. [L1–L7]
4. **Add supplemental plots where they solve a gap.** Distinguish a larger seasonal forage resource from a small staging or observation plot. Small plots may be rapidly browsed; a large food source can create exit problems. Compare available acreage, soils, flood duration, sunlight, equipment, browse pressure, crop rotation, and maintenance. Soil-test by plot and time planting around local recommendations. An exclusion cage can distinguish poor establishment from consumption. Choose the acreage after inventorying the property. MU Extension's example sizes and percentages are starting references for appropriate sites. [M2]
5. **Check the river work first.** Flag riparian buffers, wet areas, floodways, active banks and access constraints. For any bank, crossing, fill, or channel work, consult applicable county and agency sources before estimating it as feasible. Avoid major infrastructure in recurring flood paths until field and agency checks support it. [M4–M8]

## Check the results

For each pilot record the deficiency, its seasons, proposed change, comparable untreated location where feasible, time or budget, and how the decision will be revisited. Useful evidence includes changes in native browse and regeneration, screening continuity during leaf-off, treated area survival after flood, number of usable access days, and repeated daylight deer use per comparable camera effort. A higher photo count can be caused by moving a camera. Preserve dates and effort. Track age-class sightings in bands, and evaluate herd health, browse impact, crop damage and recruitment alongside mature-buck opportunities. Year-one success can be a better baseline or a cleaner route. Use several seasons before claiming a change in age structure or habitat use.
